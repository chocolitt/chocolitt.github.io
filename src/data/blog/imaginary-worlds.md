<p>One of my favorite books growing up was <a href="http://www.dougal-dixon.co.uk/">Dougal Dixon</a>'s <a href="https://www.amazon.com/Man-After-Anthropology-Future/dp/0312035608">Man after man: an anthropology of the future</a>, which imagines the development and speciation of humanity in the far future -- under the influence of both genetic engineering and apocalyptic disaster.</p>

       <figure><img src="/assets/squarespace/2f97246f60c5-image-asset.webp" alt="Somehow the genetically engineered humans of the far future have mullets." loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Somehow the genetically engineered humans of the far future have mullets.</figcaption></figure>

  <p>I looked back on the book recently and was struck by how imaginative Dixon is, but also how his imagination is limited in some ways -- the future he imagines is visibly an '80s future (see: the haircuts of the "hivers" he imagines in the picture above).  You can find a semi-legal copy of the book online <a href="http://www.sivatherium.narod.ru/library/Dixon_3/01_en.htm">here</a>.</p>
