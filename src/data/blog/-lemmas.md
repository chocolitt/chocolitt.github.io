<p>A dilemma is a difficult choice between two alternatives.  I recently learned that there is a word for a choice between <em>three</em> alternatives:  trilemma.  But what if I have a hard choice between four options?  </p><p>I was curious, so I did some googling.  It turns out that there's some disagreement as to what a choice between four options should be called -- is it a quadrilemma or a tetralemma?  (The Greek and Latin prefixes for "three" are both "tri-," so there's no conflict in that case.)  I'd argue for the Greek tetralemma, as the suffix -lemma comes from the Greek word for <em>premise</em>, and Google seems to agree:  there are 25,100 hits for tetralemma and only 6,940 for quadrilemma.  </p>

       <figure><img src="/assets/squarespace/277fb2bcaf61-image-asset.webp" alt="Here&#x27;s a dilemma: should I be spending my time on this or on something more important?" loading="lazy" /><figcaption>Here's a dilemma: should I be spending my time on this or on something more important?</figcaption></figure>

  <p>I was curious as to how this played out for more -lemmas; the Greek "tetralemma" and "pentalemma" dominate the Latin "quadrilemma" and "quintilemma," respectively.  However, the internet seems to have found the Latin-Greek mix "sexalemma" irresistible, for obvious reasons, and the Latin "septalemma" easily won out over the Greek "heptalemma."  There are a huge number of "nonalemmas," apparently, and almost no "ennealemmas." And apparently people with 100 choices prefer the Latin-Greek creole "centilemma" to the pure Greek "hectalemma."  I was unable to find either the Latin or the Greek prefix for 99, but I'm sure that for those linguists with 99 problems, this ain't one.</p><p>Some interesting multilemmas:</p><ul><li><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis%27s_trilemma">The Lewis Trilemma</a>:  the argument that Jesus was either "Lunatic, Liar, or Lord."  The eponymous Lewis is C.S. Lewis of Narnia fame, who was also a famous Christian apologist.</li><li><p>The Charleston Mercury argued, reported the <a href="http://www.nytimes.com/1861/07/24/news/quadrilemma.html">New York Times</a> in 1861, that the Confederate States were caught on the horns of a quadrilemma:  their options were to negotiate, engage in privateering, fight on the sea, or use the economic power provided by the cotton trade to forward their interests.</p></li><li><p>On the other hand, the "<a href="http://surface.syr.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1006&amp;context=phy">non-classical logic of India</a>" preferred the tetralemma, or catuskoti, which was the claim that a statement could be either true, false, neither, or both(!).  This seems to be an originally Buddhist idea which has made its way into other parts of Indian philosophy.</p></li><li><p>Perhaps following Lewis, contemporary Christian apologists have come up with quintilemmas and other myrialemmas.  The pentalemma seems to be largely of interest to online dictionaries.</p></li><li><p>Please don't google "sexalemma."  On the other hand, there are several interesting hexalemmas -- for example, <a href="http://www.research.ed.ac.uk/portal/files/12473197/BROWN_C_Better_never_to_have_been_believed_Benatar_on_the_harm_of_existence.pdf">this paper</a> by Campbell Brown argues that it is better to exist than not, rebutting previous depressing work of Benatar (David, unfortunately, not Pat). Brown postulates the existence of a person, named Jemima, who exists in many worlds, and compares those worlds to one in which she does not exists.  He extracts six alternatives from this comparison -- if you are interested despite my description, feel free to read more.</p></li><li><p>The Buddhist tetralemma above has been extended to a <a href="https://books.google.com/books?id=6kortLAhMuEC&amp;pg=PA137&amp;lpg=PA137&amp;dq=septalemma&amp;source=bl&amp;ots=PtR1rETV0z&amp;sig=kgW9ZsU6tWIIwhUj8heR1IVmwQA&amp;hl=en&amp;sa=X&amp;ved=0ahUKEwjLnveBlMDPAhULlh4KHSMuAR4Q6AEIJjAB#v=onepage&amp;q=septalemma&amp;f=false">septalemma</a> by the Jains.  <a href="https://books.google.com/books?id=_XqBCgAAQBAJ&amp;pg=PA179&amp;lpg=PA179&amp;dq=heptalemma&amp;source=bl&amp;ots=Tfl41-wLCs&amp;sig=_D3T-NbNC-EdhMAsWktyAmk3Sp4&amp;hl=en&amp;sa=X&amp;ved=0ahUKEwiU5rGflMDPAhXBFx4KHZZMAAQ4ChDoAQghMAE#v=onepage&amp;q=heptalemma&amp;f=false">Other literature</a> refers to it as a heptalemma.</p></li><li><p>Apparently <a href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/1206.2403.pdf">the problem of time in quantum gravity</a> leads to an octalemma.</p></li><li><p>And most of the hits for "decalemma" are the result of Google generously interpreting my search as an interest in "decal Emma." </p></li></ul>
